SAP EWM POSC vs LOSC Storage Control: A Practical S/4HANA Guide

Understand how SAP EWM splits a movement into several warehouse tasks using process-oriented (POSC) and layout-oriented (LOSC) storage control, and how to choose, configure and troubleshoot each.

A pallet arrives at the dock, needs to be unloaded, checked, deconsolidated and then put away to a high-bay rack through a conveyor. If SAP EWM created a single warehouse task from the door to the final bin, none of those stops would be visible, controllable or confirmable. Storage control is the EWM mechanism that breaks one logical movement into several steps.

This guide explains SAP EWM POSC vs LOSC storage control: what each method does, how they differ, where they are configured, how they combine, and what usually goes wrong in projects. By the end you should be able to choose the right method for a given warehouse scenario, sketch the customizing, and answer the typical interview questions on it.

The concepts apply to both embedded EWM in S/4HANA and decentralized EWM. Where the menu path or behaviour is something you should verify in your own release, I say so.

⚡ Key takeaways
  • Storage control splits one logical movement into several warehouse tasks, each confirmed separately.
  • POSC models business steps such as unloading, inspection and deconsolidation; LOSC models physical routing through intermediate storage types, sections or bins.
  • POSC and LOSC can be combined: POSC for the process, LOSC for physical transit points within a step.
  • Most failures come from missing determination settings or missing bin master data, not from the rule itself.
  • Test the full cycle with /SCWM/MON, /SCWM/TO and /SCWM/TO_CONF before transporting, and keep the number of steps minimal.

01What storage control means in SAP EWM

By default, when EWM creates a warehouse task (WT) for a putaway, it moves the product from a source bin to a destination bin in one go. Storage control lets you insert intermediate steps, so the movement becomes a chain of warehouse tasks. Each task is confirmed separately, usually by a different person or resource, and often in a different area of the warehouse.

EWM offers two ways to define those intermediate steps:

  • Process-oriented storage control (POSC) – you define the steps by business process: unloading, counting, quality inspection, deconsolidation, packing, loading and so on.
  • Layout-oriented storage control (LOSC) – you define the steps by physical warehouse layout: the product has to travel via a specific intermediate storage type, section or bin, such as a conveyor, an identification point or a lift.

The simplest way to remember it: POSC answers "what must happen to this goods?" and LOSC answers "which physical route must it take?"

ECC vs S/4HANA: In ECC WM there was no real equivalent. You used interim storage types and two-step movements manually. Storage control in EWM is available in both embedded and decentralized EWM, and the concepts are identical. Check the embedded EWM setup walkthrough if you need the system architecture first.

In practice, POSC is the more common starting point in projects. LOSC appears when the warehouse has real physical constraints: automated storage, narrow-aisle zones with transfer points, or separate floors.

03How process-oriented storage control works

POSC splits a process into external steps and internal steps. External steps are the business activities that are visible to the outside (for example unloading, deconsolidation or counting). Internal steps are the system-level technical steps in between. You group them into a storage process and assign that to the warehouse movement through the warehouse process type and determination settings.

Worked example: inbound with inspection and deconsolidation

Assume a mixed pallet arrives from a vendor in Pune. The business needs to unload, deconsolidate the mixed pallet into single-product HUs and then put them away.

  1. Storage process Z_INB1 is defined with the steps: unloading, deconsolidation, putaway.
  2. The goods receipt is posted into the GR zone (storage type e.g. 9010).
  3. EWM creates the first WT for the movement from the door to the deconsolidation work centre.
  4. When that WT is confirmed, the next WT is created from the work centre to the final putaway bin.

Each step has its own WT, so the stock position always tells you exactly where the HU is. The open tasks sit in /SCWM/ORDIM_O and confirmed ones in /SCWM/ORDIM_C.

Where to configure it

The path starts in SPRO > SCM Extended Warehouse Management > Extended Warehouse Management > Cross-Process Settings > Warehouse Task and then the node for process-oriented storage control. The exact node names vary slightly between releases, so open the node list in your system and verify. You will typically work through:

StepWhat you define
1Storage process steps (external and internal) and their allowed combinations
2The storage process (sequence of steps)
3The determination of the storage process for a movement, together with the warehouse process type
4Warehouse process types for each step if different handling is needed
Tip: Name your storage processes by business meaning, for example Z_INB_QI for inbound with inspection, not Z001. When you are three years into the project, nobody will remember what 001 was.

04How layout-oriented storage control works

LOSC is about physical routing. You tell EWM that goods travelling from storage type A to storage type B must pass through an intermediate storage type, section or bin. The intermediate stop might be an I-point where a pallet is scanned and checked, a conveyor entry, or a transfer bin of a lift.

Worked example: automated high-bay

Products going to the high-bay storage type 0200 must first go through the conveyor entry point 0900.

  1. Putaway searches the destination bin in 0200 as normal.
  2. Because a layout-oriented rule exists for the route from GR zone to 0200, EWM inserts the intermediate step via 0900.
  3. WT 1 moves the HU from the GR zone to the conveyor entry bin.
  4. WT 2, normally triggered after confirmation or by the automation interface, moves it into the rack.

In an automated warehouse, the second step is often not confirmed by a person at all. The material flow system (MFS) or a PLC communicates the completion. If you are designing that, involve the automation vendor early.

Where to configure it

Settings sit under the same Warehouse Task area in SPRO, in the layout-oriented storage control node. You define, per combination of the relevant storage types and conditions, the intermediate storage type, section and bin, and the allowed sequence. Be very exact with the master data: storage types, sections and the intermediate bins must exist (/SCWM/LS01 creates storage bins) and the bins must be allowed for the product or HU type.

Warning: LOSC steps go wrong most often because of missing bin master data or wrong storage-type roles. The rule is correct, but the system finds no suitable intermediate bin, and the WT fails or falls back to direct putaway.

05Combining POSC and LOSC in one warehouse

Large distribution centres use both. POSC defines the business process steps that matter for documents and control; LOSC then adds physical transit points inside a step where the layout needs them.

Example: pharma distribution centre

  1. POSC defines: unload, quality inspection, putaway.
  2. After quality inspection is released, the putaway step targets an automated mini-load area.
  3. LOSC adds the conveyor entry and lift transfer bin for that destination.

Result: three business steps, but four or five physical warehouse tasks. The business sees three milestones; the operators see the actual physical hops.

Design guidance

  • Start with POSC to model the process. Add LOSC only for real physical constraints.
  • Do not create a POSC step just to represent a conveyor. Use LOSC.
  • Do not use LOSC to represent a business activity such as inspection. That belongs in POSC.
  • Keep the number of steps minimal. Each step adds a WT, a confirmation and a possible failure point.
Interview angle: "The customer wants a conveyor transfer between the goods receipt area and the rack. POSC or LOSC?" The answer is LOSC, because it is a physical layout route. Then add that if there is also an inspection requirement, that part is POSC.

06Testing storage control step by step

Before you hand over, simulate a full cycle in a sandbox. If you are practising on an S/4HANA sandbox, use this order:

  1. Create an inbound delivery and post goods receipt in the delivery processing.
  2. Check the created WT with /SCWM/TO or via the warehouse monitor /SCWM/MON. Confirm that only the first step has been created.
  3. Confirm the WT, either in /SCWM/TO_CONF or through the RF framework (/SCWM/RFUI).
  4. Check that the next WT has been created automatically and that its source is the destination of the previous task.
  5. Repeat until the HU reaches the final bin and stock is in the correct storage type.
  6. Test the exceptions: cancel a WT, put a quantity difference in a step, and test a bin that is full.

You can also create a WT manually for a HU using /SCWM/ADHU, which is handy for isolating whether the problem is the storage control determination or the putaway strategy.

Tip: In the monitor, look at the HU's current location and the warehouse task list together. If the HU is stuck in an intermediate bin and no follow-up WT exists, the issue is nearly always in the determination, not in the stock data.

08Interview questions on this topic

  1. What is the difference between POSC and LOSC? POSC models business process steps (unload, inspect, deconsolidate). LOSC models physical routing through intermediate storage types, sections or bins.
  2. Can you use both together? Yes. POSC gives the process framework and LOSC adds physical transit stops inside it.
  3. How many WTs are created for a three-step process? One per step, created in sequence as each is confirmed, unless the design says otherwise.
  4. Where would you look if the second step is not created? Determination settings, the warehouse process type, the step sequence, and the open tasks in the monitor.
  5. Is storage control different in embedded and decentralized EWM? The concept and customizing are the same. The difference is the system landscape, as discussed in the embedded vs decentralized decision matrix.
Interview angle: Give an example from a project, even a small one. Saying "in our case the pallet had to pass an I-point, so we used LOSC" is worth more than a textbook definition. More practice questions are in the top 25 EWM interview questions.

?Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between POSC and LOSC in SAP EWM?

POSC defines intermediate steps by business process, such as unloading, counting or deconsolidation. LOSC defines them by physical layout, such as routing via a conveyor, I-point or transfer bin.

Can POSC and LOSC be used together?

Yes. Many warehouses use POSC for the overall business process and add LOSC where the physical layout needs an intermediate stop inside a step.

Is storage control available in embedded EWM in S/4HANA?

Yes. Storage control works in both embedded and decentralized EWM, and the customizing concepts are the same. The landscape differs, but the warehouse task logic does not.

Which T-codes help to check storage control results?

Use /SCWM/MON for the warehouse monitor, /SCWM/TO to display warehouse tasks, /SCWM/TO_CONF to confirm them and /SCWM/ADHU to create a WT for a handling unit manually.

Why is the second warehouse task not created after I confirm the first?

Typical causes are a missing next step in the sequence, a missing determination for the warehouse process type, or missing or blocked intermediate bin data. Check the open tasks and the monitor first.

Should a beginner learn POSC or LOSC first?

Start with POSC, as it is more common and follows the business process. Learn LOSC once you are comfortable with storage types, sections and bins, since it depends on layout master data.
